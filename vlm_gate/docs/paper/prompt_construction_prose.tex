\subsection{How the question sets were written}
\label{app:prompt}

The label we need is a judgement, not a measurement: whether the stretch of motion
ahead of a given moment could be executed in fewer, longer steps without changing
the outcome. Rather than asking a VLM this question directly, we decomposed it into
a handful of concrete checks that a camera can answer -- is the gripper closing on
its target right now, is the approach threaded through nearby objects, is the
object already resting where it belongs, is the arm carrying something through open
space -- each graded on a 1--5 scale, signed as a risk or a safety cue, and weighted;
the confidence is the signed, normalised sum $c=\tfrac12\bigl(1+\sum_q s_q w_q g_q\bigr)$
with $g_q=(\mathrm{grade}_q-1)/4$ and the weights on each side summing to one. The
wording of these checks was drafted and revised interactively with
\textbf{Claude Opus~5} (Anthropic). The model was given the definition of the target
above, the phase structure of each benchmark's tasks (reach, grasp, carry, place,
retreat, derived from gripper events), sampled demonstration frames at chunk
boundaries together with the per-chunk quantities the prompt later states as facts
(whether the gripper opens, closes or stays put in the window, the direction and
speed of motion relative to the dataset's own speed distribution, and whether the
arm is decelerating to a stop), a small set of chunks we had graded by eye, and, as revisions accumulated, the per-phase
firing rate of every check and the closed-loop retention of each phase under
compression. Its job was to turn observed failure modes into wording -- a check
that fired on the approach as well as on the grasp was narrowed to ``the fingers
already around it and only the squeeze left''; a check that never fired on any phase
was dropped; benchmark-specific hazards (a mug on a rack, a bowl threaded out from
between two dishes, an object reached into a microwave) were written into the
guidance rather than left to the grader -- and to keep the text within what a
single image pair can support. Every revision was re-graded on the same fixed probe
set and accepted only if the paired ranking of anchor chunks improved beyond its
bootstrap interval, so that changes inside the noise were not kept. The result is
one prompt per benchmark (35--41 lines each, reproduced verbatim below); they share
the same instruction skeleton and differ in the guidance paragraph and in which
four to six checks are asked.

The full datasets were then labelled with \textbf{Gemini~3.8~Flash} through the
Gemini API. Each demonstration was cut into 16-frame chunks; for every chunk the
grader received the camera views of the chunk's first frame (scene and wrist views
for LIBERO, the two agent views and the wrist view for RoboCasa), the task
instruction, the computed facts for that chunk, and the question set, and returned
one integer grade per check (the API exposes no token probabilities, so the grades
are the model's argmax choice). Confidence was computed from the grades with the
frozen signs and weights, attached to the chunk's frames as a per-frame target by
interpolating between chunk anchors, and frames outside the labelled span were
masked out of the confidence loss. The fixed probe set was graded again inside
the full pass and compared with its grades from the last revision round, to confirm
that the labels were stable under re-sampling. At roughly \$3 per thousand chunks,
labelling RoboCasa (7{,}200 episodes, 131k chunks) and LIBERO (1{,}693 episodes,
16k chunks) cost a few hundred dollars in total, and the same pipeline was reused
unchanged for the real-robot datasets.
