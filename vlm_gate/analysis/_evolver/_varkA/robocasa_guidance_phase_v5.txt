Decide whether the next second of motion can be run at half rate, by merging each pair
of steps into one command.

There are five things to look for, and each one is a different way the merge can hurt.

Most of the time the gripper is simply travelling through empty space, touching nothing
at all. A longer step changes nothing there, whatever the task is called. This is the
common case and it merges safely.

The gripper is holding a part that can only move one way. A handle, a knob, a lever, the
edge of a door or drawer: once the gripper closes on one of these, the arm must follow
the path that part is built to travel - a drawer slides straight out, a door swings on
its hinge, a knob turns in place. A merged step cuts the corner off that path, and what
the arm cannot turn into motion becomes a pull on the fingers, so the grip slips or the
part jams. This is why closing a drawer survives merging while opening the same drawer
does not, though the drawer and its friction are the same either way.

Pushing is the opposite. A gripper pressing a button, a panel, or a door that is being
pushed shut holds nothing, and the surface takes up anything extra. A merged step that
goes too far just presses a little harder.

The load is passing between the hand and the world. Closing onto an object until its
weight is taken, and setting an object down until it stands on its own, are the moments
when a few millimetres decide whether it is held or dropped. Carrying it afterwards, with
the grip unchanged, is not delicate at all - it is the transfer that is.

The path has to bend. Carrying a loose object is safe in the open, and becomes risky where
the way is not straight: a rim to lift over, a shelf edge or a wall to go around, a lip at
the mouth of a sink or a cabinet. Merging replaces the corner with a straight line across
it, and the object clips what it should have cleared.

The arm alone cannot reach. When the target sits at the far edge of the workspace, the
base has to travel while the arm works, and the two must stay in step. Merging doubles
both, but the base and the arm do not accept the same size of command, so the timing that
kept the target within reach comes apart.
