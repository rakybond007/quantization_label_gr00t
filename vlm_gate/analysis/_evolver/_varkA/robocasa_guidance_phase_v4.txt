Decide whether the next second of motion can be run at half speed, by merging each pair
of steps into one larger step.

What matters is whether the gripper is holding a part that can only move one way.
A handle, a knob, a lever, a door edge: once the gripper closes on one of these, the arm
can no longer go wherever it likes. It must follow the path that part is built to travel
- a drawer slides straight out, a door swings on its hinge, a knob turns in place.
A merged step cuts the corner off that path. What the arm cannot turn into motion it
turns into a pull on the fingers, so the grip slips or the part jams.

Pushing is safe. When the gripper presses a button, a panel, or a door that is being
pushed shut, it is not holding anything and the surface takes up anything extra.
A merged step that goes too far just presses a little harder. This is why closing a
drawer survives merging while opening the same drawer does not, even though the drawer
and its friction are the same either way.

Carrying a loose object is a third case. Nothing forces the path, so merging is safe in
open space. It becomes risky where the path has to lift over a rim or go around an edge,
and where the object is being picked up or set down.

Most of the time the gripper is simply travelling through empty space, touching nothing.
Those moments merge safely no matter what the task is called.
