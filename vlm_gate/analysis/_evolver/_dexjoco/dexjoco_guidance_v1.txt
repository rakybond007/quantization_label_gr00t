Decide whether the next second of motion can be run with some of its commands left out.

This robot is commanded by absolute poses: every command says where the arm and the
sixteen finger joints should be. Leaving commands out does not make the motion smaller,
it makes the robot jump further between the commands that remain. Nothing is averaged
away; the arm simply travels the gap in one move and the intermediate poses never happen.

That is harmless when the gap is empty. Reaching toward something before touching it,
carrying it across open space, withdrawing afterwards - the hand ends up where it was
going either way, and a few millimetres of overshoot on the way costs nothing.

It is not harmless in four situations, and they are what to look for.

The hand is doing the work with its shape. When the fingers are wrapped around a tool
and their arrangement is what grips it or operates it, a longer jump lets that
arrangement drift and the tool turns or slips inside the hand. Holding a pair of tongs
and squeezing them is the clearest case: the grip and the task are the same motion.

The thing being handled can only move one way. A hinge, a lid, a folding arm: once the
hand is on it, the path is decided by the object, not the robot. A jump cuts across that
path, and the force that cannot become motion pushes against the hand instead.

A contact has to land on a small exact spot. Driving a nail means the head is a target a
centimetre wide, and the swing has to arrive there. Missing by the amount a longer jump
adds is the difference between a hit and a miss.

The tilt of what is held decides the outcome. Pouring from a can, tipping something over:
the angle at each moment is the thing being controlled, and skipping angles pours in the
wrong place.

Pressing is the forgiving case. When the hand pushes a button or a surface, going too far
just presses harder, and the surface absorbs it.
