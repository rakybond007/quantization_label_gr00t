Decide whether the next second of motion can be run at half rate, by merging each pair
of steps into one command.

These commands are end-effector deltas, so merging two steps means executing their sum.
The controller clips each dimension at plus or minus one. A single step never reaches
that limit in this data, but a merged pair does on about 8% of pairs, and whatever is
clipped off is displacement the arm simply never travels.

Most of the time the gripper is crossing empty space, holding nothing or carrying
something with room around it. A longer step changes nothing there, whatever the task is
called. This is the common case and it merges safely.

What decides the rest is not the motion but where the object has to end up.

A destination can be forgiving or unforgiving. Dropping something into a basket or a bowl
is forgiving: the container catches it, and landing a centimetre off changes nothing.
Setting something down on a plate, or onto a burner, is not: it has to sit squarely on a
small flat target, and the last approach is where a merged step puts it down askew.

An enclosed space is its own case. Moving an object into or out of a drawer, or onto a
shelf inside a cabinet, means clearing an opening on the way. Merging replaces the path
around the lip with a straight line across it, and the object catches on what it should
have cleared.

The moment the load transfers is decisive on its own. Closing onto an object until its
weight is taken, and setting it down until it stands by itself, are moments where a few
millimetres decide whether it is held or dropped. Carrying it afterwards, with the grip
unchanged, is not delicate at all.

Clutter matters for the same reason a rim does. When the gripper reaches in among other
objects standing close by, a longer step is a wider swing, and something that was clear
gets knocked.
